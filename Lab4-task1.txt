?- house = house.
true.
why:Both terms are identical , so unification succeeds.

?- house = abc.
false.
why:Two distinct constant atoms (house and abc) cannot unify.

?- edge(a,b) = edge(C,b).
C=a.
why:Both terms have the same functor (edge/2); the second arguments match (b = b), and variable C unifies with atom a.

?- edge(a,d) = edge(C,B).
C=a,
B=d.
why:Same functor (edge/2); variable C unifies with a and variable B unifies with d.

?- edge(C,B) = edge(a,d).
C=a,
B=d.
why:Unification is symmetric; variable C unifies with a and B unifies with d.

?- edge(A,f(g,h)) = edge(a, B).
A=a,
B=f(g,h).
why:Same functor (edge/2); variable A unifies with a, and variable B unifies with the compound structure f(g,h).

?- edge(A,A) = edge(b,c).
false.
why:The variable A cannot simultaneously unify with two distinct constants (b and c).

?- edge(A,A) = edge(b,b).
A=b.
why:Both arguments on the right are identical (b), so variable A unifies with b.

?- A=B, B=f.
A=b, B=f.
why:A and B alias together first. When B is bound to f, Prolog displays the binding chain A = B, B = f. to show A is linked to B.

?- A=B, B=f, A=e.
false.
why:A and B are bound to atom f; attempting to subsequently unify A (f) with e fails because f and e are distinct constants.
